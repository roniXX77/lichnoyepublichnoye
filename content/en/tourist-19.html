German speech mixed with Russian in American airports has become an unpleasant, irritating backdrop to thought. I sit on a little blue seat and bite into an apple. A man gave it to me at the airport yesterday. When I was little my parents taught me not to take candy from strange men. Now between candy from strange men and my stupidity a great big gulf has opened – bigger than hunger, wider than the gaps between the little airport seats. I was late for my flight, and the two of us were standing at security. He was forty or fifty, with his own company in Atlanta and a desire to help me in a difficult moment, more like a dodgy, pathetic creep than an eccentric, to be honest. I told him I was flying home from my boyfriend’s. And said I hadn’t eaten since morning. Right in front of the officers he took out a plastic bag with three green apples. We agreed to meet in California. I bite into the apple on the reasoning that I might very well be lucky enough to avoid getting poisoned. Nobody told me in childhood that if you size up the motives of strange men who give you phone numbers and apples, you can turn a blind eye to the rules. In the end nobody needs you. You’re no Snow White and you’re not even his type. Has nobody considered the version where Snow White could have killed herself? Or rather that’s exactly what it was – a deliberate decision to take a bite, to be biblical and pure for a moment before sinning with the words «maybe i’ll get away with it». Yesterday I missed my flight. With a plastic bag in my hands from a very strange man. I look at the mesmerizing patterns of teeth in the apple – what have I done? Thank god I was late and missed my chance to see Detroit. The world protects me. The patterns on the apple are colored with blood and bring on a feeling of early-morning preflight excitement. His earlobes fill with blood at once, like heavy scarlet dewdrops. I don’t know why, but his ears are always a little reddish, as if from shame or fear. When my grandmother taught biology in her little town she always took me to the greenhouse where the crickets and praying mantises lived. I was a sickly child and afraid of nature because I didn’t know it. I endured it and went red in my ears, cheeks, nose. I was ashamed and scared, as maybe he is now. I turn his face with such care that he’ll be able to hear me. We agreed to play the quiet game, so I can’t make myself heard any other way. Big green-blue eyes open onto me. They look a lot like earlobes: skittish, tender, honest, very childlike. They look like childhood. And smell of wind off the sea. I touch the tip of his nose and the bridge. Its surface is firm and smooth in the poetic sense – it makes you want to scribble on it in pencil. Scribble about how I want you to wake up and read my scribbles with your airy eyes, or how I want sleep to be unnecessary and your eyes to be open onto me always, always, and to see them from just far enough away to touch the tip of your nose. I sit, snow-white (Snow-White-ish), on the same little blue seat where I waited for your flight from San Francisco. I’m very alone. I’m very much an orphan. Somewhere three hours from here, in his jeering bachelor apartment, I left my oatmeal dried onto the bottom of a saucepan; an empty vodka bottle and two dirty glasses; ropes scattered on the floor; glasses of old water all around the bedroom; a pack of pantyliners in the bathroom cabinet. I have so much to tell the world about you and the grubby little landscape of time we painstakingly built, like a microcosm of some action two weeks long. To tell how at the very tip of that landscape you tied my hands with a bit of rope and told me I wasn’t going anywhere, because I cried while I was packing. The plane’s delayed, but this time I’m definitely flying away.
