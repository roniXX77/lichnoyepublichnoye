Of the few things my vast inner Russia taught me, here is one – to stay alone. The spring of twenty twenty-two was my first notice of death. They told me: "Write a dispatch, a situation report in three dimensions", and I started writing.About how they told me, no, how they confessed to me in a whisper with their eyes buried in the March snow: "You’re going to be all alone in this enormous country". They told me I was going to be alone, and I didn’t understand how that could be, how the fuck that could be possible, how there could be a reality where only one person was left in Russia, and that person was weak, scrawny and hungry. I thrashed in the corners, I squeezed myself into the stuffiness of my own geometry, dreaming of getting out, dreaming of running away. Naive. Not noticing with my happy eyes his mother’s awkward smile as I tell her about my phantom move, looming in the fog of a Khrushchev-era balcony. Collecting little tears and words of encouragement at metro stations from men passing by who don’t understand, don’t know that soon I’ll be here alone. They’ll all become little soldiers, lucky ones or little corpses, and I’ll stay alone. Then my tender creature Russia looked into my watercolor eyes and said: "You can run away from the inside, but we’ll still be together". I wrote to her: "Then I’d rather stay alone" – and quit giving a shit about breaking down the state borders between Verona and Moscow. In the summer of twenty twenty-two I left that cold rotten country and locked myself inside my Russia. Funnily enough there was more air – more fields, more open space and less fucking thought. They told me: "It’s already over, we have to stop", and I left Moscow, wiping away her tears with a grubby little white handkerchief. Then I looked my parents in the eyes and said: "I’m alone now" Then I looked my friends in the eyes and said: "I’m alone now" Then I looked strangers in the eyes and shouted: "I’m alone now" And I left Moscow with one of those strangers, wiping away her tears with a grubby little white handkerchief. I wrote in Yekaterinburg rain on my white Moscow skin that I love going to somebody else’s apartment first thing with flour, milk and cigarettes, that I love buying cans of spray paint, that I love cutting collages out of Russian newspapers. Here’s what’s strange: I used to live in a world where everything felt like home, and I was afraid to be alone. And then I left with a stranger for his strange Russian fairy tale, fell in love with it, fell in love with him – but I stayed living in a world of strangers, and in a world like that it’s easier to breathe alone. I got used to it, I went soft, I stuck to the walls of Russian cities in blotches and buff marks, I got used to it, I went soft, I got used to being alone, I went soft from my own selfhood, to all those strangers I stayed archaic too and a stranger again. I made a habit of smoking and laughing, smoking and laughing, smoking and laughing, smoking – and leaving for a Russia that wasn’t mine, for people who weren’t mine, when Moscow rubbed my eyes especially raw with painful shards of memories of home. In the fall of twenty twenty-two I was beaten up, kissed on my cold forehead and told: "We need to take a pausze, I’m leaving", strangers told me: "We’re strangers, but we have to stop being close", a stranger spat in my face on the day of the first snow and said: "Even I, a stranger, can leave you alone, even I, from another Russia, can break that callousness you’ve acquired inside, you idiot, you creature, you whore, you whore who belongs to no one and nowhere". I wrote to him: "Then I’d rather not wait for you to come back" – and quit giving a shit about breaking down the borders between the Urals and Moscow, between what was mine and what was foreign. I quit giving a shit that borders existed in my vast Russia – swallowing the salty bitterness into an empty north wind, realizing I could be alone without defining what was mine and what was foreign, for the first time I looked that tender creature Russia in the eyes again and left for her alone. I left for Petersburg alone, I became – alone – so I could leave for him, I stopped being a tourist in my own country when I became its ubiquitous nomad. And so I went soft and got used to us being together, but separately. Me and Her, side by side in the same broth of grief, rasping laughter and peeling walls. In February of twenty twenty-three I remembered that I hadn’t ended, and began gutting myself, cutting up sick thoughts, screaming in unison with strange Russian walls, no, howling alongside the little soldiers and their little wives. I got scared of losing this world again, even if I was alone in it, I tried to fix everything, I tried running to my Russia so she would steal me away – in desperation I brought the stranger to her altar so she would take him in, caress him, help us save ourselves and break down the borders, and she rejected us both. I returned to the cycles of the northern capital and became a fucking dirty tourist. Lying on the stained icy bed, naked, torn to pieces, we became two indifferent, washed-out tourists, and I grew sick of it and scared to be anything but alone again. Scared to be a tourist instead of a nomad, especially in a herd of other equally dead tourists. The spring of twenty twenty-three was my first. Just like that. I said: “We need to take a break, I’m leaving”, but to spring I whispered, pressing my lips to hers – you’remyfirst – and she and I were the first to fly to a strange city to love naively, because cities full of strangers are made for feeling – you’remyfirsti’myourfirst – and we flew over Russia together and knew that very well.
