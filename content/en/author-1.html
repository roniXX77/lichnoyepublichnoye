Act I Scene one. i’m thinking about clothes. clothes don’t make the person /then what, what does?/ what colors a person is the goosebumps of a text running over the skin, sometimes a person paints herself black or bright-bright scarlet, when the heart turns liquid and tastes bitter, cars make a person, achievements, flowers behind the ear, the variety of the journey and eternal youth /hallelujah/ i’m thinking about clothes and how at some point they started painting me in the grubby colors of the past and staining, staining, staining, staining me with something foreign bitch, you were going to write a screenplay what kind of screenplay is this it doesn’t look like a director’s cut if anything. a little less than. you need to come up with actions, not just! be a builder write the fucking once upon a time there was a white shirt and it lived, it has to be said, a very short life. when it was a child, it liked going to the store and buying itself cotton candy, snuggling and squirming against the skin of family life, it liked combing dolls’ hair and catching it on its sticky cotton skin. when it grew young, nothing ordinary or futurely happened, because a shirt was a shirt and it hadn’t the slightest idea, unlike its owner, that it had a shelf life of its own, short as a button loop. when the shirt grew young its owner had already almost grown old from his youth and shot himself. /gunshot/ /gunshot/ /gunshot/ /he loves me he loves me not no – he shot himself/ /period/ the shirt stained itself in blood so it could remember there was at least this – little spots, little loops, tender little moments – a smudge – it stained itself, and so it would never get clean again, it threw itself away. this isn’t a screenplay, it’s some sort of fairy tale for children! you can see nobody cares, everyone’s read this a hundred times by age five, and then again in school. you have to think of something if not original then at least not quite so banal and naive. idiot. think, write, format. faster, higher, stronger. /i’d smirk right now if i weren’t a text/ format. get a move on, try harder, you have to show everybody you can write screenplays, you already promised you were a director, but what kind of director are you when your texts are little bricks made of the familiar and tiresome. all the fairy tales have been told. all the stories have been learned by heart over tea in the kitchen at night. i could go down to the store now and get some fresh cigarettes. this week-old pack and i are almost the same – neither of us exactly fresh, neither making any claims to quality /though we should/ right, pull yourself together, you rag. get it together. you got distracted again. write a screenplay. start with the characters at least. Author. (smiling slyly, but sad and closed off around the eyes) But I didn’t come back here today to tell fairy tales. Obviously nobody’s needed or cared for that in a long time. Still, just think – hardly a banal situation – I got to thinking about clothes and the way they stick to my skin. Yesterday I tried to pull on a costume of last year’s me – you know, silly, a little sad, but only out of childhood naivety, stupidly in love with schoolboys – stuck a Hawaiian shirt, a bucket hat and the dull light of eyeshadow onto myself. I thought I could still snag a ticket for the last trolleybus in the Urals, you know, feel like a girl just once more /a giiirl/ silly enough to collect heaps of receipts and colorful cigarette packs. A year ago I liked music and knew how to dance. A year ago shorts and kissing boys in high school really suited me (laughs awkwardly). It’s so absurd. You probably see an aging woman in front of you now with ideas beside the point and wonder how she can say she was that silly only a year ago, and that her clothes are to blame (clears her throat and turns serious again). And still I remember a great deal. I hold on too hard. I liked it when those clothes fit me just right. I believed I wouldn’t outgrow them in a year, but no – already too small. (aside, annoyed) Already all patched up and rough. (loudly, to the audience) Through years of wars, pointless losses and little newspapers nobody’s read in ages, I’ve outgrown shorts and school kisses, bright joy and colored hard candy in my pupils. (spreading her arms) These things happen, you can’t run from them! But I didn’t know it would turn out like this, and I don’t know if it’s good – to understand that last year’s clothes are too small for you. Audience. That story isn’t interesting at all. (the author stays silent and smiles) Can’t you pick something more entertaining or at least appealing, since you’re getting us all together here? (the author stays silent) (louder) You promised to tell us a screenplay! You promised a movie! How can you just promise a movie without good reason? (the author looks around awkwardly) Come on, please, do something at least, just once something with a shape and a scrap of dignity. (the murmur swells, the light focuses on the author) Tell us something at least! Read a poem, if everybody hasn’t forgotten them. They have, haven’t they? Callousness. Vileness. You have to write a screenplay! You hear? Write! Write a screenplay! Just write some sort of digestible screenplay with characters other than yourself. Write! A screenplay! Wri! ting! The way! Creation and callousness! Do the bare minimum in the brief! Write at least a little something to touch the soul – poems always look like poems, you say, so where are these poems, where are the poems, where are the poems – (the murmur drops sharply. silence falls) Author. (shrinking back, face pale and crooked-lipped, swallowed) accept any state even if the little vial went out even if your heels are shaking even if it’s the mirage of a coat burnt right through and you’re riding the metro and that’s your first fight with the world – you know there the anti-tank hedgehog shoots its spikes into you on the sly there, where you want to return but none of us is holy enough, to refuse to accept any state in vials, in pills, in stairwells, on rooftops and inside the sea you’re not cold enough to keep from shaking with fear but you’re not honest enough to come home (the author falls off the chair and dies. they carry her away.) Scene two. With the agent in love. The Agent in Love comes onstage, pink and light, with mesh on his body and hair the color of soft pastel. He’s wearing a linen dress, quite contemporary and wavelike. He drags the Author’s body by the arms, dressed in a Hawaiian shirt, knee-length shorts and a bucket hat, pulls it to center stage and sits on the body in place of a chair. Agent in Love. (quietly, honestly) And today they gave me new clothes and said: “Now you’ll live a new life. Get ready for a new uniform” – well, you know, like conscripts getting issued army boots, and they, those conscripts, understand they’ll keep wearing totalitarian boots for a long, long time, quite possibly to the very end. Getting used to the uniform, getting used to a new form of life – is that all of me now, then? They issued me clothes a size bigger – I tried them on, they turned out oversized, I drowned in them a little – but it’s not so bad really. I grew one centimeter this year (stands up, pretends to measure her height), so I’ll grow into this new form somehow (to herself). Even if I’m already an old lady. They gave me new clothes and said now I had to leave and be new too, learn to become new on demand when something ends. At first I protested and tried to undress, but (pauses to light a cigarette) nudity doesn’t suit me, and to my surprise these slightly too-big clothes had stuck to my skin, just as Hawaiian shirts and being in love stuck to me a year ago. Clothes for newness and the unknown. A nomad’s clothes. /looks herself over, smiles/ Just right. Can you imagine? Only clothes – they’re like plates. While the little blue ball spins and turns, we sit in our own plates and keep our heads down, and it seems to us that it fits us just right. (stands) But I don’t like being in my own plate, because there are too many of them. I don’t like jumping from one broth to another before it gets cold. I don’t like shedding my skin every time, and like her here /points to the Author/ dropping dead in my own plate, even if it’s a different plate from the one a year ago. Honestly, I take offense at all this cycling around and at my own fate acting like a dictator. I’d like to last a little longer than a year and find out what there is past the porcelain rims and glass domes. (looks around) Anyway, it’ll soon be time for us to go too. We have to leave before the boy gets here. Children – you know, such strange alien creatures. (leaves the stage, dragging the Author’s body along) Act II. Scene one. The Author is sitting in the front row watching the soundless credits on the screen. A boy of about 10–12 comes up to her. In his hands are a compass, a spyglass and a map of Japan. Boy. Lady, what’s your name? Author. My name is Author. Boy. Did you film the story about the clothes? Author. I did. Boy. You did a great job (looks at the credits through the spyglass). I just wanted to know, you said this was your first screenplay which is dedicated – but to whom? (shrugs with his little hands) To whom? Author. (looks at the Boy, smiles quietly) Who do screenplays get dedicated to? (with a touch of sadness, turning to the Boy with a piercingly honest look) This screenplay was dedicated to something else.
