When I was a little under twenty, I happened to discover that I was a very stupid person, so I promised myself I’d start reading good books – about sincerity, about intellect, about the magnitude of life. I settled on Oksana Vasyakina’s «Steppe». I thought autofiction was fashionable and great, though I’d started reading the same novel a year earlier and found everything except the occasional erotic scene too cloying and chewed over. I opened a book for the first time in years, though as a child I’d read serialized novels, Isaac Asimov and Chekhov with rapture in my grandmother’s empty summer house. Opening the book was like a pitying orgasm against my will – the slightly bitter realization that I really was stupid. I read the novel about the steppe at an unhurried pace and with a scholarly air, trying to notice literary devices and explain them in the dry muffled voice of a schoolmarm. Resentment was swelling inside me: how does she remember childhood so well, possess it the way I don’t? To be here for a reason, unlike the shells in her steppe, to be because she owes it to her sensitive memory. I felt ashamed and envious in the face of fashionable Vasyakina. The stupidest thing of all – is looking for literary devices in life. A month later I opened the book to read its last chapter. That evening rain spilled over Los Angeles – it was a great happiness. My roommate and I pulled on jackets that looked out of place among the palms, like fancy dress, and went out to dance in the downpour. Cold from the rainy air blowing in through the wide-open balcony door, I sat awkwardly hunched by an ancient heater from the forties. Its little coils glowed red and gave off an uncomfortable heat, drying my skin, pink from the fresh air. I turned the pages patiently, switching the heater off every so often to feel an unpleasant chill, then switching it back on and suffocating in the heat all over again. The warmth of the heater, the old apartment falling apart, the smell of printer’s ink from the book – it all reminded me of evenings in Kursk, where I, a defenseless useless child with no internet and no parents, had the good fortune to lose myself in books and breathe in the barnyard smell of the household at the same time. Suddenly my thoughts felt a fresh, new acceleration: memory fell on me like a sharp tin can, and its thawed contents poured over me. My grandfather’s death, Mom and Grandma fighting, news of the bombing of Kursk, Grandma’s village school where everyone knew me as the Russian teacher’s granddaughter, my bewildered arrival in Kursk at eighteen trying to escape a desperate teenage love, wildflowers, the sundresses Mom bought me just for the village, sharp-tasting strawberries and the cold columns of the half-empty dacha, and the pigeon’s nest on top of one of those columns, and the «Tricolor» satellite dish, and watering the roses in the evenings, and watching festival films in Grandma’s study until six in the morning... It all washed over me as it never had before: it turned out to be separate from me, but inside me. I felt like a woman assembled out of distinct, sensitive bits of speed, process, feeling. I felt homeless and sure of what I knew. I wanted to talk loudly about my precision and sharpness. I turned – as this one and that one and the next had turned – twenty years old again. I became a person.
